Welcome to the desk

FinOps: the discipline that makes the negotiation worth winning

A great cloud contract on a wasteful estate is still a bad outcome. This guide teaches FinOps — how organizations see, allocate, optimize, and govern technology spend — explained plainly, with pictures, and always connected back to the deal.

Coach's opening note

If the Cloud Negotiations Playbook is about the price, this one is about the quantity. Cloud cost is always rate × usage. Negotiation attacks the rate once every few years. FinOps attacks both, every single month. Clients who get this right walk into renewals with clean data, a defensible forecast, and no waste baked into the commitment — which is exactly the client a principal wants to represent.

The mental model: rate × usage

💲 Rate — "pay less for what you use"

Commitment discounts (Savings Plans, Reservations, CUDs), enterprise agreements, licensing benefits, and negotiated credits. Usually owned centrally by the FinOps team and procurement.

📉 Usage — "use less of what you pay for"

Rightsizing, turning things off, cleaning up waste, better architecture. Owned by engineering, because only engineers can change what's running.

Analogy: the household electricity bill

You can shop for a cheaper tariff (rate) or you can turn off the lights in empty rooms (usage). Switching tariffs once a year is a one-time win; teaching the whole family to flip the switch is a habit. FinOps is building that habit across a company of thousands — and knowing who left which light on.

Foundations

FinOps 101 — what it is, and what it isn't

FinOps is an operational discipline and a cultural practice. It is not a tool, not a cost-cutting project, and not something finance does to engineering.

The definition that matters

FinOps (Cloud Financial Operations) is the practice of bringing financial accountability to the variable spend model of cloud, so engineering, finance, product, and procurement teams make business trade-offs between speed, cost, and quality — together, and with data.

The goal is not to spend less. The goal is to maximize business value per dollar. Sometimes that means spending more — on a launch, on a region, on AI capacity — because the return justifies it.

Why FinOps exists at all

In a data center, spend was decided by a few people in procurement, a few times a year, with purchase orders. In the cloud, any engineer with a keyboard can commit company money in seconds — and nobody sees the invoice for 30 days. Decentralized buying power plus delayed, unreadable bills is the problem FinOps solves.

Old world (data center)Cloud world
Who spendsProcurement, via POsEvery engineer, via API calls
When spend is decidedAnnual capacity planContinuously, minute by minute
Cost shapeFixed, depreciated CapExVariable OpEx that changes daily

The six FinOps principles

These are the Foundation's principles — the "constitution." When a client's practice feels stuck, one of these is usually being violated.

1. Teams need to collaborate

Finance, engineering, product, and procurement work together in near real time. No throwing reports over the wall.

2. Business value drives technology decisions

Judge spend by what it returns (unit economics), not by the total. A rising bill can be good news.

3. Everyone takes ownership for their technology usage

Accountability is pushed to the edge — the team that runs it owns its cost, the way they own its uptime.

4. FinOps data should be accessible, timely, and accurate

Cost data is shared openly and fast. A monthly PDF is not a feedback loop; a daily dashboard is.

5. FinOps should be enabled centrally

A central team sets standards, buys commitments, negotiates rates, and builds the tooling — so engineers can focus on usage.

6. Take advantage of the variable cost model

Variable cost is a feature. Scale down, use spot, buy commitments only for the baseline — don't recreate the data center in the cloud.

Coach's note

Proxima usually enters through procurement or finance. The engagement succeeds or fails on whether you win over engineering. Speak their language: "this recommendation is safe to apply, here's the evidence, and here's how it affects your SLO" beats "you're over budget" every time.

Watch out: FinOps is not a cost-cutting project

A one-off "cloud cost reduction" sprint gets savings in quarter one and gives them back by quarter four, because nothing changed about how decisions are made. If a client frames FinOps as a project with an end date, reframe it as an operating capability — or expect the waste to grow back.

The framework

Phases, domains & capabilities

The FinOps Foundation Framework is the shared vocabulary of the industry. Learn its three layers and you can place any client conversation on the map in seconds.

Layer 1 — The lifecycle: three phases on a loop

Where are we?

Inform

Visibility and allocation. Get the data in, attribute every dollar to an owner, forecast, budget, and benchmark.

→
What could we do?

Optimize

Find the opportunities: rate optimization (commitments, discounts) and usage optimization (rightsizing, waste).

→
How do we sustain it?

Operate

Turn opportunities into action: policy, automation, governance, cadence, and continuous improvement.

↺ Operate feeds back into Inform — the loop never ends, and teams run it at different speeds for different capabilities.

Analogy: getting fit

Inform is stepping on the scale and tracking what you eat. Optimize is choosing the diet and the training plan. Operate is actually going to the gym every week, and adjusting when it stops working. Skip Inform and you're guessing; skip Operate and you're on a crash diet.

Coach's note

The phases are not a waterfall. A client can be "Run" on commitments but "Crawl" on allocation. In practice, allocation (Inform) is the bottleneck for almost everyone — you can't hold teams accountable for costs you can't attribute to them.

Layer 2 — The four domains

Domains are the outcomes a FinOps practice exists to deliver. Each groups a set of capabilities.

🔎 Understand Usage & Cost

Gather and structure the data so everyone knows what is being spent, where, and by whom.

📈 Quantify Business Value

Connect spend to plans and outcomes: forecasts, budgets, benchmarks, and unit economics.

⚙️ Optimize Usage & Cost

Improve efficiency on both the rate and the usage side, plus architecture, licensing, and sustainability.

🏛 Manage the FinOps Practice

Run FinOps as a function: governance, education, tooling, chargeback, onboarding, and assessment.

Scopes — FinOps is no longer just public cloud

The 2025 Framework added Scopes: the same phases and capabilities applied to other technology spend with a consumption or subscription shape. Common scopes include:

☁️ Public Cloud

The original home of FinOps — AWS, Azure, GCP, OCI.

🧠 AI

Tokens, GPUs, provisioned model throughput. Fast-growing and poorly allocated.

💳 SaaS & Licensing

Seats, consumption SaaS (Snowflake, Datadog), and BYOL software.

🏢 Data Center

Colo, private cloud, hardware — allocated with the same discipline.

🗂 Other Cloud+

Any spend with variable, usage-based cost that benefits from ownership and allocation.

🎯 Why it matters

It aligns FinOps with how Proxima already works: across the whole IT supplier estate, not one bill.

The framework

Maturity — Crawl, Walk, Run

The Foundation's maturity model is deliberately simple. It's assessed per capability, and the right target isn't always "Run."

The three stages

🐣 Crawl

Little reporting and tooling. Measurement gives some insight. Basic KPIs exist. Processes are reactive and depend on a few individuals. Fixing the easy, obvious things.

🚶 Walk

The capability is understood and followed across the organization. Hard edge cases are identified but often deferred. Automation and processes cover most of the requirements.

🏃 Run

Understood and followed by all teams. Difficult edge cases are addressed. Very high goals set and met. Automation is the default approach.

Illustrative maturity markers

MeasureCrawlWalkRun
Spend allocated to an owner< 50%~80%> 90%
Forecast variance vs. actual~20%~15%< 12%
Commitment coverage (eligible, steady-state)~60%~70%~80%+
Anomaly detectionFound on the invoiceAlerts, manual triageRouted to owner within a day
Cost reportingMonthly, finance onlyWeekly, by business unitDaily, in engineers' tools
Accountability modelNone / central team onlyShowbackChargeback + unit economics

Numbers are directional markers drawn from Foundation maturity guidance and common practice, not hard thresholds. Workload variability changes what "good" coverage looks like.

Coach's note: "Run" is not always the goal

Maturity should match the size of the prize. A client spending $2M a year on cloud should not build a Run-stage chargeback engine — the process costs more than it saves. Recommend the lowest maturity that captures most of the value for each capability, and invest in Run only where spend is large and volatile.

How to run a quick maturity assessment

Step 1

Pick the capabilities that matter

Usually 6–8: allocation, reporting, anomaly management, forecasting, rate optimization, workload optimization, policy & governance, chargeback.

Step 2

Interview each persona

Finance, engineering leads, procurement, and the FinOps lead (if one exists). Ask for evidence, not opinions: "show me the report."

Step 3

Score current vs. target

Crawl/Walk/Run today, and the target given spend size and business goals. The gap is the roadmap.

Step 4

Prioritize by value and effort

Tie each gap to dollars. Allocation gaps block everything else; commitment gaps are usually the fastest money.

Quick wins — high value, low effort

Idle resource cleanup, commitment coverage on steady compute, storage lifecycle policies, dev/test scheduling.

Strategic bets — high value, high effort

Allocation overhaul, chargeback, unit economics, architectural modernization.

Fill-ins — low value, low effort

Minor tag hygiene, small report tweaks, niche service rightsizing.

Avoid — low value, high effort

Building custom tooling that a native tool already provides; perfect allocation of trivial shared costs.

Inform

Allocation & visibility — who spent what, and why

Everything in FinOps rests on this. If you can't attribute a dollar to an owner, you can't forecast it, optimize it, or charge it back.

Step one: get the data in

The raw material is the provider's detailed billing export — not the invoice PDF.

FOCUS — the Rosetta Stone for bills

FOCUS (FinOps Open Cost & Usage Specification) is an open standard, stewarded by the FinOps Foundation, that defines common column names and meanings for billing data — BilledCost, EffectiveCost, ListCost, ChargeCategory, ServiceName, CommitmentDiscountId and so on.

v1.0 went GA in 2024 and later releases expanded coverage (including SaaS and invoice reconciliation). AWS, Azure, Google Cloud, and Oracle all publish FOCUS-formatted exports, as do a growing number of SaaS vendors.

Coach's note

For a multi-cloud client, ask for FOCUS exports first. It turns a week of normalizing three incompatible billing schemas into an afternoon, and it's the cleanest input for Proxima's baseline and diagnostic tools.

The four ways to look at the same dollar

Clients argue about "the number" because they're looking at different cost views. Know which one you're quoting.

Watch out: the "whose number" fight

Finance quotes billed cost, engineering sees amortized cost in a dashboard, and procurement tracks net against the commitment. All three are right, and the meeting goes nowhere. Agree one view per purpose at the start of an engagement and label every chart with it.

Allocation: the hierarchy of methods

Best
Account / subscription / project structure — one owner per account. Can't be forgotten or mistyped.
Good
Tags / labels — key-value metadata on resources (cost center, app, env, owner).
OK
Rules & virtual tagging — allocation rules in a FinOps tool that fill gaps after the fact.
Last
Proportional spread — distribute the unallocated remainder by a key. Honest, but nobody owns it.

Watch out: untaggable spend

Some costs can never carry a tag: support fees, marketplace charges, some data transfer, shared Kubernetes clusters, commitment purchases, credits. A client proudly reporting "95% tag compliance" may still have 20–30% of spend unallocated. Always measure allocation as % of dollars, not % of resources.

Shared costs: splitting the bill fairly

Even split

Divide equally across consumers. Simple and defensible for small, stable shared costs (e.g. a security tooling fee).

Proportional

Split in the same ratio as each team's direct spend. The most common default.

Fixed / driver-based

Split by a measured driver: CPU requests on a Kubernetes cluster, GB stored, API calls. Fairest, most effort.

Forecasting, budgeting & anomalies

📊 Forecasting

Trend-based forecasts plus known drivers (launches, migrations, decommissions). Forecast accuracy is what makes a commitment size defensible in a negotiation.

🎯 Budgeting

Budgets per owner, set from the forecast, reviewed monthly. Alerts at thresholds (e.g. 80% / 100% / forecast-to-exceed).

🚨 Anomaly management

Detect unexpected spend spikes daily, route to the resource owner, record the cause. A runaway job caught on day 1 costs a thirtieth of one caught on the invoice.

Unit economics — the grown-up metric

Total spend tells you how much. Unit cost tells you whether it's good.

Unit cost = allocated cloud cost ÷ business driver   e.g. $ per order · $ per active customer · $ per 1K API calls · $ per GB processed
Analogy: the delivery company

A courier's fuel bill doubled. Disaster? Not if deliveries tripled — fuel per parcel fell by a third. Unit economics is how FinOps stops leadership from panicking at a growing bill that's actually a thriving business, and how it catches a flat bill hiding a worsening one.

Inform · Manage the practice

Showback & chargeback — making cost someone's problem

Allocation tells you who spent it. Showback and chargeback decide what happens next: do teams merely see their costs, or do they pay for them?

The definitions

👀 Showback

Teams receive a report of the costs they drove — no money moves. Visibility creates awareness and peer pressure. Low friction, fast to launch.

💸 Chargeback

Costs are formally billed to the team's budget via internal cross-charges in the general ledger. Real financial accountability — and real political friction.

Analogy: the restaurant bill

A group dinner with one bill split evenly encourages everyone to order the lobster. Showback is the waiter telling each person what their order cost. Chargeback is separate checks. People order differently when it's their own card.

The accountability spectrum

No allocationOne central IT bill. Nobody knows who drives cost. Waste is invisible.
ShowbackCosts reported by team. Awareness, no budget impact. Most orgs start here.
Partial chargebackDirect costs charged; shared platform costs held centrally or shown only.
Full chargebackAll costs, incl. shared and support, billed to consumers against budgets.

Coach's note: showback first, always

Run at least two quarters of showback before any chargeback. It flushes out allocation errors while nothing is at stake, lets teams clean up before they're billed, and builds trust in the numbers. Chargeback launched on bad data teaches every engineering leader to distrust FinOps — permanently.

Worked example: splitting a shared Kubernetes cluster

A shared cluster costs $120,000/month (nodes, control plane, load balancers). Three teams run workloads; the driver is reserved CPU requests.

TeamCPU requestsShareAllocated cost
Payments480 vCPU40%$48,000
Search360 vCPU30%$36,000
Analytics240 vCPU20%$24,000
Idle / unrequested capacity120 vCPU10%$12,000 → platform team

Keeping idle capacity as a visible platform-team line — instead of spreading it — gives the platform team a reason to right-size node pools. Spread it, and the waste disappears into everyone's bill.

The classic chargeback traps
  • Charging before allocation is trusted: the first month is all disputes and no behavior change.
  • Penalizing commitment buyers: if the central team keeps all RI/SP savings, teams see on-demand prices and learn nothing about efficiency.
  • Charging for what teams can't control: billing a product team for a platform decision breeds resentment, not savings.
  • Mid-year budget shocks: introduce chargeback at the annual budget cycle, so teams are funded for the costs they'll now carry.
  • Over-engineering: allocating a $400/month shared fee across 60 cost centers with a bespoke driver.

The two numbers that run the portfolio

Coverage

eligible usage covered by commitments ÷ total eligible usage

Too low = paying on-demand for steady workloads. Leaving money on the table.

Utilization

commitment hours used ÷ commitment hours purchased

Too low = paying for commitments nothing is using. Burning money.

Low coverage · high utilization

Under-committed. Safe to buy more — every new commitment will be used.

High coverage · high utilization

The sweet spot. Maintain, ladder renewals, watch for workload shifts.

Low coverage · low utilization

Wrong commitments. Bought the wrong family/region; exchange or let expire and re-buy flexibly.

High coverage · low utilization

Over-committed. Stop buying; find workloads to absorb it; use exchange/marketplace options.

Coach's note

This is why 3-year commitments aren't as scary as clients think: a deep discount tolerates a lot of under-use. The real risk isn't the discount rate — it's the workload disappearing (a migration, a divestiture, a move to containers or a different family). Size 3-year commitments to what will certainly still exist, and use 1-year or flexible plans for the rest.

Portfolio strategy

🪜 Ladder the purchases

Buy in small tranches monthly or quarterly instead of one big purchase. Expirations spread out, so you can adjust to change without a cliff — and you're never forced into a large renewal decision at a bad moment.

🧱 Layer by flexibility

Most rigid, deepest discount (RIs, resource CUDs) for the stable core. Flexible plans (Compute SPs, Flex CUDs) for the middle. On-demand and spot for the spiky top.

🏛 Centralize purchasing

One team buys, at the payer/billing-account level, with a standing mandate and coverage target. Decentralized buying leads to duplicate, fragmented, poorly utilized commitments.

🔄 Optimize before you commit

Rightsize and clean up first. Committing to oversized instances locks in the waste for three years at a discount.

The layered coverage model

Spiky
Peaks, experiments, batch → on-demand & spot

Likely-to-persist but shifting → 1-yr flexible plans
Steady

Getting out of a bad commitment

ProviderExit / adjustment options
AWSConvertible RIs can be exchanged for equal-or-greater value. Standard RIs can be sold on the RI Marketplace (restrictions apply). Savings Plans generally cannot be cancelled or exchanged — the key reason to ladder them.
AzureReservations can be exchanged, and cancelled for a refund within a rolling annual cap. Microsoft has tightened exchange rules for compute reservations in favor of savings plans — confirm current policy before relying on it. Savings plans cannot be cancelled.
Google CloudCUDs cannot be cancelled. Resource-based CUDs can be merged or split across projects via sharing within the billing account. Plan with the least room for error.

Commitments and the enterprise agreement

Coach's note: where commitment management meets negotiation

Commitment purchases generally count toward the enterprise commitment — AWS Savings Plans and RIs burn down a PPA; Azure reservation and savings plan purchases burn down a MACC. And the negotiated discount usually applies on top of the commitment rate. So the commitment portfolio and the enterprise deal must be modeled together: the forecast that sizes one also sizes the other. Double-counting growth in both is the classic over-commitment.

Watch out: third-party commitment managers

Automated commitment services (and reseller-held "pooled" commitments) can deliver strong coverage with little effort — but check who owns the commitment, how fees are calculated (often a share of savings against on-demand, which overstates value), and whether provider rule changes on commitment sharing affect the model. AWS tightened RI/Savings Plan sharing for resellers in 2025.

  • Measure coverage and utilization separately, per vehicle, every month.
  • Rightsize before buying; never commit to the waste.
  • Ladder purchases in small tranches; avoid expiration cliffs.
  • Match flexibility to certainty: rigid for the core, flexible for the middle.
  • Allocate commitment benefits to the teams that used them (amortized).
  • Model the commitment portfolio together with the PPA/MACC forecast.
Optimize · Workload optimization

Usage optimization — using less of what you pay for

Rate optimization is a spreadsheet exercise. Usage optimization changes what's running — which means it only happens when engineers trust the recommendation and have time to act on it.

The waste taxonomy

💤 Idle resources

VMs at near-zero CPU, databases with no connections, load balancers with no targets. Delete or stop.

📦 Orphaned resources

Unattached disks, old snapshots, unused elastic IPs, forgotten test environments. Pure waste, low risk to remove.

🐘 Oversized resources

Instances sized for a peak that never comes. Rightsize to observed utilization with headroom.

🌙 Always-on non-prod

Dev and test running 168 hours a week when people work ~50. Schedule them off nights and weekends.

🗄 Wrong storage tier

Rarely read data on hot storage. Lifecycle policies to infrequent-access and archive tiers.

🕰 Legacy generations

Old instance families cost more for less performance. Modernize to current-gen or ARM (Graviton, Cobalt, Axion).

Watch out: the order of operations

Rightsizing an instance that's covered by a Reserved Instance can strand the reservation — the smaller instance no longer matches, and the RI goes unused. Coordinate usage and rate optimization: flexible commitments (Savings Plans, Flex CUDs) tolerate rightsizing; rigid ones don't.

Why recommendations don't get done

Every client has a tool showing hundreds of thousands of dollars in "recommendations." Most never happen. The reasons are predictable:

No owner

Can't route the ticket without allocation. Back to §04.

No trust

Engineers have been burned by a naive rightsizing that ignored memory or peaks. Show the evidence behind every recommendation.

No time

Cost work competes with features. Put it in the sprint, not in an email.

No incentive

If savings don't show up in the team's own view (showback), nobody feels the win.

Coach's note

Present savings as a ranked, owned backlog, not a total. "$1.2M of opportunity" is noise. "Team Payments: 14 items, $180K/yr, top 3 are low-risk and take a day" is a plan an engineering manager can say yes to. Report realized savings monthly so the program earns credibility.

Building cost into engineering

  • Cost in the pull request — infrastructure-as-code cost estimates before changes merge.
  • Architecture reviews include a cost section for new services.
  • Default-off non-prod — environments are scheduled unless someone opts out.
  • Guardrails, not gates — policies block the egregious (e.g. largest GPU instances in dev) and warn on the rest.
  • Efficiency as a quality metric alongside latency and availability.
Operate

Governance & operating model — making it stick

Operate is where most FinOps programs stall. The data is there and the opportunities are known; what's missing is the team, the cadence, and the policies that turn insight into habit.

The FinOps team: where it sits

Reports intoStrengthWeakness
CFO / FinanceBudget authority, forecast rigor, procurement linksSeen as "cost police" by engineering; weak technical credibility
CIO / CTO / Cloud PlatformEngineering trust, can automate and enforceCan under-invest in forecasting, allocation, and finance integration

Whatever the reporting line, the Foundation's consistent finding is that successful practices have an executive sponsor, a dedicated central team that enables rather than does, and named FinOps champions embedded in engineering teams.

Watch out: buying a tool instead of building a practice

A FinOps platform is a multiplier on a process that already exists. Bought first, it becomes a dashboard nobody opens. AI, SaaS & beyond cloud

The same Inform → Optimize → Operate loop now applies to the fastest-growing, least-governed spend in the enterprise: AI and consumption-based SaaS.

FinOps for AI — why it's different

🔤 New units

Input and output tokens, images, audio minutes, GPU-hours, provisioned throughput units. Pricing differs by model, context length, and caching — and changes frequently.

⚡ Scarce capacity

GPUs are supply-constrained. Capacity reservations and provisioned throughput trade flexibility for guaranteed access, often with steep minimums.

🧪 Experimental spend

Many teams, many pilots, many API keys — often on corporate cards or outside the cloud bill entirely. Allocation is weak by default.

📉 Value is uncertain

Unit economics (cost per resolved ticket, per generated document) matter more than ever, because ROI is still being proven.

Coach's note

Start AI FinOps with allocation, not optimization: one gateway or account structure so every token is attributable to a team and use case. Then attach a business metric. Then optimize. It's the same order as cloud — clients just skip it because the spend feels experimental.

Watch out: AI commitments

Provisioned throughput and GPU reservations are commitments with the same break-even math as a Savings Plan — but model generations turn over far faster than instance families. A 12-month reservation on a model that's superseded in six is a stranded commitment. Keep terms short and insist on model-portability or exchange rights when negotiating.

FinOps for SaaS & licensing

Seat-based SaaS

Inform: license inventory and usage. Optimize: reclaim inactive seats, right-tier editions. Operate: joiner/leaver automation.

Consumption SaaS

Snowflake, Databricks, Datadog: credits and ingest volumes behave like cloud. Allocate by warehouse/workspace/team; commit to baselines.

Licensing in cloud

BYOL and hybrid benefits (e.g. Azure Hybrid Benefit), license-included vs. bring-your-own pricing, and audit exposure from vCPU-based licensing.

FOCUS-formatted exports from SaaS vendors are making it possible to put these costs on the same dashboard as cloud — which is exactly where the Foundation's Scopes are heading.

Sustainability — the allied discipline

Carbon and cost are closely correlated: idle resources, oversized instances, and hot storage for cold data waste both. Provider carbon tools (AWS Customer Carbon Footprint Tool, Azure Emissions Impact Dashboard, Google Cloud Carbon Footprint) let a FinOps program report emissions alongside spend. Useful for winning sponsorship from ESG leaders — and increasingly a reporting obligation.

In the field

FinOps meets the deal

Where this playbook and the Cloud Negotiations Playbook join. FinOps is what makes a commitment defensible, a forecast credible, and a discount real.

Why FinOps is negotiation prep

1. A clean baseline

Optimize before negotiating, or you commit to the waste. The baseline should be the post-optimization run-rate, not today's bill.

2. A credible forecast

Commitment size is a forecast. Allocation by business unit plus known drivers makes the number defensible — to the CFO and to the vendor.

3. A commitment portfolio in view

Existing RIs/SPs/CUDs and their expirations shape how much enterprise commitment is safe and when to sign.

4. Governance the vendor can see

A client that can prove it hits its commitments gets better terms, ramps, and flexibility — it's lower risk to the provider.

The FinOps-to-renewal timeline

T minus 12 months

Assess & allocate

Maturity assessment; get billing exports (ideally FOCUS); push allocation above ~80% of spend; stand up showback.

T minus 9–12 months

Optimize the estate

Waste cleanup, non-prod scheduling, rightsizing. Capture the run-rate reduction before it's baked into a commitment.

T minus 6–9 months

Forecast & model commitments

Bottom-up forecast by business unit; model the commitment portfolio and enterprise commitment together; set coverage targets.

T minus 3–6 months

Hand off to negotiation

Baseline, forecast, and scenarios feed the negotiation strategy. Hold large commitment purchases that would lock in position early.

Close

Contract to operations

Translate the contract into FinOps terms: commitment burn-down tracking, credit expiry dates, price-protection checks, true-up dates.

Ongoing

Operate & prepare the next one

Monthly burn-down vs. commitment, coverage/utilization, and a data trail that makes the next renewal easier.

The classic traps at the FinOps–negotiation seam
  • Locking in waste: signing the enterprise commitment on the pre-optimization bill.
  • Double-counting growth: the same growth used to size both the RI/SP portfolio and the PPA/MACC.
  • Buying commitments mid-negotiation: large purchases just before signing hand the vendor the spend without the concession.
  • No owner for the contract after signing: credits expire unused, burn-down drifts, price protection goes unchecked.
  • Optimizing after committing: a successful rightsizing program that pushes the client below its commitment floor.

Sourcing the FinOps work — options when you don't own a practice

Proxima doesn't run a FinOps delivery practice today. That's as much a positioning choice as a gap: the independence that makes our commercial advice credible is exactly what most FinOps delivery models compromise. This section is the menu — who can do the work, who pays for it, and where the conflicts hide.

Coach's framing

Split the work in two before you decide anything. The commercial half — baseline, allocation standards, forecast, commitment strategy, contract — is sourcing work Proxima already does. The engineering half — rightsizing, scheduling, tagging enforcement, platform tooling, automation — needs hands in the client's cloud accounts. You almost never need to own both to run a valuable engagement; you need a credible answer for who does each.

How VARs and resellers actually get paid

The client rarely sees this, and it explains almost every behavior you'll observe from a partner-led FinOps offer.

💱 Resell margin & partner of record

Under the hyperscaler channel programs (AWS Solution Provider, Microsoft CSP, Google Cloud reseller/MSP), the partner buys cloud wholesale and bills the client, keeping a spread or rebating part of it. "Free FinOps" is funded out of that spread — the client pays for it in the rate.

🎁 Provider funding

Hyperscalers pay partners to run assessments and migrations: AWS MAP and Optimization & Licensing Assessments, Microsoft's Azure migration/modernization funding and ECIF, Google's migration and partner incentive programs. This is genuinely cheap for the client — the provider is paying — but it comes with a migration or consumption agenda.

🧾 Services & platform fees

A managed FinOps service on top of a licensed platform (often priced as a percentage of cloud spend under management — a fee that grows with the bill).

📊 Gainshare

A share of "savings," usually measured against on-demand list or a pre-engagement run-rate. The baseline definition is the entire negotiation.

Watch out: the four conflicts to name out loud
  • Margin vs. mandate: a partner earning a percentage of consumption has a structural reason not to shrink it. Ask how their commercial model survives a 25% reduction in the client's bill.
  • Partner of record: moving billing through a reseller can change eligibility for private pricing, support terms, and who sits in the room at renewal. Never let a client change partner-of-record status mid-negotiation without modeling the effect on the PPA/MACC.
  • Pooled commitments: some MSP models hold commitments in their own name and pool the benefit. Check whose balance sheet the commitment sits on and what happens at exit — AWS tightened reseller RI/Savings Plan sharing in 2025, which broke several of these models.
  • Savings math: a fee taken as a share of savings against on-demand list charges the client for discounts they already had. Insist the baseline is the actual pre-engagement run-rate.

Coach's note: a shortlist worth building

If Proxima wants two names on the bench rather than a market map, pick for complementarity, not coverage: one specialist consultancy for engineering depth on the Optimize/Operate side, and one commitment-automation vendor for rate-side wins we can't execute ourselves. Due diligence on any FinOps partner

Whether you're referring, subcontracting, or reviewing a partner the client already has, ask these. The answers sort the genuinely independent from the margin-funded fast.

  • Do you resell or manage any of this client's cloud, and what margin or rebate do you earn on it?
  • How is your fee calculated — against list price, on-demand, or the client's actual pre-engagement run-rate?
  • Who holds the commitments, in whose name, and what happens to them if we terminate?
  • Who owns the billing data, the tooling configuration, and the allocation rules at exit?
  • Are you a FinOps Foundation Certified Service Provider, and which named staff hold current certifications?
  • Will you contractually leave the client's direct provider relationship and private pricing untouched?
  • Show realized savings on a comparable estate — net of your fee, measured against a real baseline.
  • Does your recommendation engine ever favor a product or provider you resell?

If Proxima wants to build the capability

🎓 Train (cheapest, slowest)

Put existing consultants through FinOps Foundation certification (Certified Practitioner is the entry point, plus role-specific tracks) and join the Foundation. Gives vocabulary and credibility on the Inform and rate side — not engineering depth.

🤝 Partner formally

A named delivery partner with a reciprocal referral and subcontract agreement, so Proxima can prime an engagement and staff the engineering half on demand. Fastest route to a credible offer.

🧑‍💻 Borrow

An associate or contractor bench of FinOps practitioners, white-labelled. Variable cost, no bench risk, but quality depends on individuals.

🏗 Hire a lead

One senior practitioner paired with existing sourcing consultants seeds a practice. Only worth it with a pipeline of engagements to fund the bench.

Full glossary

Allocation
Attributing cloud costs to owners (teams, products, cost centers) through account structure, tags, and rules.
Amortized cost
Cost view that spreads upfront and recurring commitment fees over the term and across the resources that benefited.
Anomaly
An unexpected change in spend versus a learned pattern — usually a runaway resource, misconfiguration, or unplanned launch.
Billed / unblended cost
Cost as charged on the invoice, at the time it was charged.
Break-even utilization
The minimum use of a commitment that beats on-demand pricing: 1 − discount rate.
Chargeback
Formally billing technology costs to the consuming team's budget via internal accounting entries.
CCoE (Cloud Center of Excellence)
A cross-functional team that sets cloud standards, architecture patterns, and governance — often home to FinOps.
Commitment-based discount
A lower rate in exchange for committing to usage or spend for a term: Savings Plans, Reserved Instances, Reservations, CUDs.
Committed Use Discount (CUD)
Google Cloud's commitment discount — resource-based (vCPU/memory) or spend-based (Flex).
Convertible RI
An AWS Reserved Instance that can be exchanged for different attributes of equal or greater value.
Coverage
The share of eligible usage covered by commitment discounts.
Crawl / Walk / Run
The FinOps Foundation's three-stage maturity model, assessed per capability.
CUR (Cost & Usage Report)
AWS's most detailed billing dataset, now delivered through AWS Data Exports.
Domain
One of the four outcome areas in the FinOps Framework, each grouping several capabilities.
Effective Savings Rate (ESR)
The blended percentage saved versus on-demand across all rate optimization — the best single rate KPI.
FinOps
Cloud Financial Operations — the practice of maximizing business value from technology spend through collaboration between engineering, finance, procurement, and product.
FinOps Certified Service Provider
A FinOps Foundation designation for consultancies and managed service providers that meet its training and practice requirements — a useful (not sufficient) filter when vetting a delivery partner.
FinOps Foundation
The Linux Foundation project that maintains the FinOps Framework, FOCUS, and certification programs.
Gainshare
A fee taken as a share of measured savings. Its value depends entirely on the agreed baseline — actual pre-engagement run-rate, not on-demand list.
FOCUS
FinOps Open Cost & Usage Specification — an open standard schema for billing data across providers and SaaS vendors.
Inform / Optimize / Operate
The three iterative phases of the FinOps lifecycle.
Laddering
Buying commitments in small, regular tranches so expirations are staggered and risk is spread.
List / on-demand equivalent cost
What usage would have cost at public list price — the reference point for measuring savings.
Net amortized cost
Amortized cost after negotiated discounts and credits — the truest cost of ownership view.
Partner of record
The reseller or partner attached to a cloud account under a provider channel program (AWS Solution Provider, Microsoft CSP, Google reseller). Changing it moves billing, margin, and sometimes negotiating position.
Persona
A role in the FinOps model — practitioner, engineering, finance, procurement, product, leadership — plus allied personas.
Provisioned throughput
Reserved AI model capacity (e.g. Azure OpenAI PTUs) priced per unit-hour regardless of token use.
Rate optimization
Reducing the price paid per unit — commitments, negotiated discounts, licensing benefits.
Rightsizing
Matching resource size to observed need, with appropriate headroom.
Savings Plan
AWS and Azure's flexible commitment to an hourly spend amount for 1 or 3 years.
Scope
A category of technology spend the FinOps Framework applies to — public cloud, AI, SaaS, licensing, data center.
Shared cost
A cost consumed by multiple owners (platforms, support, networking) that must be split by a chosen method.
Showback
Reporting costs to the teams that drove them, without moving money between budgets.
Spot / preemptible
Deeply discounted spare capacity that the provider can reclaim at short notice.
Tag / label
Key-value metadata attached to a cloud resource, used for allocation, automation, and policy.
Unit economics
Cost expressed per unit of business value — per customer, transaction, or product.
Usage optimization
Reducing the quantity consumed — idle cleanup, rightsizing, scheduling, architecture.
Utilization
The share of purchased commitment actually used.
Virtual tagging
Allocation rules applied in a FinOps tool to attribute untagged or untaggable spend after the fact.
Final word from your coach

Remember three things: allocation comes first, optimize before you commit, and FinOps is a habit, not a project. Clients who internalize those walk into every renewal as the best-prepared buyer the vendor will see all year — and that's the client you want to be negotiating for.
